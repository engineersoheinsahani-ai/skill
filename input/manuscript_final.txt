ABSTRACT
====================================================================
Machine learning is now the default tool for predicting residential
building energy use, yet three problems undermine its use in retrofit
prioritisation and policy analysis: pipelines are rarely audited for
data leakage; models report point estimates without calibrated
uncertainty; and end-use disaggregation is physically inconsistent
with total site energy. We address all three in a single pipeline
built on the U.S. Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020
(18,496 households, 799 variables), enriched with climate features
from 1,020 TMY3 stations. The pipeline derives 47 physics-informed
features from building geometry and thermodynamics, then trains an
energy-conserving multi-task architecture: a primary model for
log-total site energy, four share models for end-use fractions, and
adaptive per-task routing frozen on validation. On held-out
households the audited pipeline attains R2 = 0.64 in original energy
units (0.67 population-weighted; 0.76 out-of-fold in log space).
Split-conformal intervals calibrated on an untouched 5% split reach
79.5 / 90.2 / 96.4% empirical coverage at the 80 / 90 / 95% nominal
levels. A nine-test leakage audit executes on every run and emits a
machine-readable report; a full re-execution sixteen days later
reproduced every headline metric. Leave-one-climate-out experiments
(mean R2 = 0.70; every household held out exactly once) confirm
transfer across climate regimes. Across ten random seeds, knowledge
distillation from a ResStock 2025.1 teacher (494,974 simulated
buildings) yields no effect distinguishable from seed noise. The
teacher never sees survey targets. For retrofit decision-support we
show that naive survey-based savings estimates can flip sign due to
population-composition bias (air sealing: -8.7% naive vs. +2.1%
composition-corrected), and we provide a matched-cell correction. We
offer no R2 record. We offer a pipeline whose errors, limits, and
null results are measured.

1. INTRODUCTION
====================================================================
Machine learning now dominates building-energy research, and with
good reason. Reviews catalogue successful deployments across load
forecasting, HVAC control, fault detection, and occupancy modelling,
with reported improvements of 10-40% over traditional engineering
approaches in specific contexts (Hong, Wang, Luo and Zhang, 2020;
Hong and Li, 2026). In the U.S. residential sector — where buildings
consume roughly one fifth of national energy — accurate prediction
of total and end-use consumption underpins retrofit prioritisation,
programme targeting, and energy-code design (Seyedzadeh, Rahimian,
Glesk and Roper, 2018). Predicting, benchmarking and validating
energy demand in the existing stock — and the advanced analytics
that make such predictions trustworthy for retrofit, electrification
and decarbonisation decisions — sit at the centre of this agenda.
Three gaps, however, keep the field from producing models that
decision-makers can actually trust.

The first gap is leakage. Information bleeds between training and
testing pipelines more often than the literature acknowledges:
Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) found leakage in 648 papers across 30
scientific fields. Like them, we treat leakage as a systemic rather
than incidental failure; unlike the cross-disciplinary audits they
catalogue, however, ours executes automatically on every run of a
single building-energy pipeline, and its verdict is archived with
every model. The building-energy literature has been slow to adopt
the automated reproducibility checklists now mandatory at NeurIPS,
ICML, and ICLR (NeurIPS, 2025). Where leakage is mentioned at all,
it is usually an ad-hoc manual inspection, not a version-controlled
protocol. The deficit extends to documentation: dataset datasheets,
model cards and standardised reproducibility checklists — routine at
ML venues and increasingly recommended for AI-based building science
(Hong and Li, 2026) — remain rare in building-energy papers.

The second gap is uncertainty. The vast majority of models provide
a single point estimate and stop (Xu, Wang, Hong, Miller, Li and
Ding, 2025). For retrofit decision-support, where overconfidence
leads to misallocated capital and missed savings, this is dangerous.
Ensemble modelling, Bayesian neural networks, and Gaussian processes
have all been proposed for uncertainty quantification in building
energy modelling (Xu et al., 2025), yet split-conformal prediction —
distribution-free, with finite-sample coverage guarantees and
minimal assumptions (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005) — remains
underrepresented. Similar to the applications Xu et al. review, we
adopt conformal prediction; where they typically calibrate on data
that also drove model selection, we reserve a split that no decision
has touched.

The third gap is physical consistency. End-use disaggregation is
conventionally treated as an independent post-processing step:
heating, cooling, DHW, and lighting are predicted by separate models
whose outputs are not constrained to sum to the total site energy
(Amasyali and El-Gohary, 2018). Sums can exceed totals. Naive
implementations therefore undermine trust in model-derived retrofit
scenarios.

This paper addresses all three gaps through an energy-conserving
multi-task architecture (Fig. 1) with four contributions. First, an
automated nine-test leakage audit (A-H) executes on every pipeline
run; it writes a machine-readable JSON report that we version-
control alongside model artefacts. Second, we calibrate split-
conformal intervals on a dedicated untouched split; test coverage
reaches 79.5 / 90.2 / 96.4% at the 80 / 90 / 95% nominal levels on
held-out households. Third, adaptive per-task routing between
share-derived and direct-EUI pathways, with renormalised shares,
keeps derived end-use energy below predicted total by construction;
unlike the independent end-use models reviewed by Amasyali and
El-Gohary (2018), our shares can never sum above unity. Finally, we
quantify sim-to-survey transfer learning honestly: a ResStock-
trained teacher contributes meta-features to the RECS student
without ever seeing survey targets, and the transfer effect is
bounded by ten-seed confidence intervals, including the case where
the measured effect is zero.

Throughout, the organising question is not "how high can R2 go?"
but "what can national survey microdata honestly predict, and with
what guarantees?" Put differently, we would rather report a modest
number whose error bars we trust than a large number whose audit we
cannot run. Section 2 describes the data; Section 3 the methods;
Section 4 results; Section 5 discussion; Section 6 concludes.

2. DATA
====================================================================
2.1. RECS 2020 microdata
The U.S. Energy Information Administration's Residential Energy
Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020 provides a nationally representative
cross-section of 18,496 occupied housing units with 799 variables
spanning building characteristics, equipment, demographics, and
energy billing (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2023). We
derive total site energy (TOTALBTU, kBtu) and four end-uses: space
heating, space cooling, domestic hot water, and lighting. Energy Use
Intensity (EUI) is total energy divided by conditioned floor area.
The tails are wide. Table 1 summarises the target variables and the
routing decisions frozen on the validation split. We normalise the
sampling weights (NWEIGHT) and apply them during training; we map
missing codes (e.g., -2, 98, 99) to NaN before feature engineering.

2.2. TMY3 climate features
Typical Meteorological Year version 3 (TMY3) data from 1,020 weather
stations are aggregated to 19 climate features per station,
including heating and cooling degree days (HDD65, CDD65),
temperature-percentile statistics, and solar radiation metrics
(Wilcox and Marion, 2008); we merge them to RECS households by
state. The correlation between TMY3-derived and RECS-reported HDD65
is 0.944, which confirms successful climate linkage. TMY3 is typical
by design. Extreme-event risk analysis therefore requires design-day
or future-climate inputs and lies outside the scope of this study
(see Limitations).

2.3. ResStock 2025.1 for transfer learning and external validation
The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's ResStock 2025.1 dataset
provides physics-simulated baseline and upgrade scenarios for
approximately 550,000 representative U.S. buildings (National
Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2025). We use the published national
files: 549,972 baseline rows (upgrade 0) and five upgrade scenarios
— reference HVAC (upgrade 3), cold-climate ASHP (4), air sealing
(10), attic insulation (11), and ENERGY STAR windows (15). The
upgrade files cover 100%, 100%, 56%, 92%, and 98% of baseline rows
respectively. We harmonise ResStock columns to the RECS numeric
schema.

The published ResStock files do not expose a building identifier, so
baseline-upgrade pairs cannot be joined row-wise outside NREL's own
environment. All simulation-derived savings estimates therefore use
matched-cell population deltas: for each cell of (Building America
climate zone x housing type x heating fuel) present in both the
baseline and the upgrade file, we compute the mean simulated saving
and re-weight it to the baseline population. This composition-
corrected estimator is mandatory: Section 4.6 shows that for air
sealing the naive (unmatched) population estimate and the corrected
estimate disagree in sign.

====================================================================
3. METHODOLOGY
====================================================================
3.1. Physics-informed feature engineering
We compute 47 physics-informed (PH_*) features from raw survey
attributes: building geometry (height, volume, surface-area-to-
volume ratio), envelope properties (window area, insulation
proxies), thermodynamic load estimates (heating, cooling, base
load), and interaction terms (vintage x HDD; occupancy x floor
area; HDD x floor area). All are row-local computations with no
fitted parameters before splitting. Leakage safety follows by
construction.

3.2. Splitting and preprocessing
A four-way stratified split (70/10/5/15; 12,947 / 1,850 / 925 /
2,774 households) uses the simplified Building America climate zone
(Cold, Hot, Mixed) so that every regime appears in every split. Each
split has a single, pre-registered role. The validation split drives
model selection only (early stopping, per-task routing, optional
hyper-parameter search). The calibration split is reserved untouched
for conformal quantile estimation. The test split is used solely for
reporting, after all decisions are frozen. Roles never change. We
ordinal-encode the 550 categorical variables and standardise the 151
numeric ones; every transformer is fitted on the training split
alone. Feature selection (train-only; mutual information plus domain
filtering) retains 250 features, including 43 physics-informed
features.

3.3. Energy-conserving multi-task architecture
The architecture has three components (Fig. 1). The primary model
is an XGBoost regressor for log(1 + TOTALBTU) with fixed default
hyper-parameters in the reported run; an Optuna search restricted to
the validation split is available in the pipeline but was not
enabled here, so that the reported figures are reproducible without
search stochasticity. Total energy is generally more predictable
than intensity (Wang and Srinivasan, 2017). Four share models
(LightGBM) predict end-use fractions on [0, 1]; we clip and
renormalise predictions so that the share sum does not exceed unity,
with the remainder the implicit "other/appliances" use. Derived
end-use energy can therefore never exceed predicted total. Adaptive
routing trains a direct-EUI model per end-use, selects the better
route on validation R2 (Table 1), and freezes the routing before
testing. Two caveats deserve immediate honesty. The heating margin
was 0.7045 vs. 0.7039 — a knife-edge — so we treat routing as a
selection device only. And the lighting task, whichever route is
chosen, remains the weakest (Section 5.2).

3.4. Honest stacking ensemble
To reduce variance without leakage we implement textbook stacking
(Mitchell, 1997): 5-fold out-of-fold predictions from XGBoost,
LightGBM, and CatBoost train a RidgeCV meta-learner with
non-negative coefficients; base learners are retrained on 100% of
the training data; column order is fixed. In the reported run the
coefficients are 0.10, 0.17, and 0.32 respectively, the intercept
carries the remainder. Weights are non-negative by construction. No
residual-on-validation modelling is performed, so the calibration
split remains a true holdout for conformal estimation.

3.5. Split-conformal calibration on an untouched split
Prediction intervals are calibrated exclusively on the 5%
calibration split (n = 925), which no model selection, early
stopping, or routing decision has touched:
    q_hat = the ceil((n_calib + 1)(1 - alpha))-th smallest
            absolute residual on the calibration split,
and we report intervals as [exp(mu_hat - q_hat) - 1,
exp(mu_hat + q_hat) - 1] in original energy units. Calibration
touches nothing else. Because it never sees the test set — and,
unlike three-split designs, never sees the selection split either —
test coverage is an unbiased estimate of reliability. We report
coverage at three nominal levels (80 / 90 / 95%).

3.6. Anti-leakage protocol (tests A-H)
The automated audit runs on every execution and writes a JSON
report: (A) forbidden-column screening on all four splits;
(B) single-feature correlation cap (|r| > 0.90 against any target
flags); (C) exact-duplicate train/test rows; (D) fitted-transformer
isolation — we re-derive medians and encoder categories from raw
training data and verify them against stored values; (E)
implausible-score early warning (R2 > 0.99); (F) train-only feature
selection verification; (G) linear-combination reconstruction — the
best 4-feature group is regressed on the target and must not exceed
R2 = 0.95; (H) target-derived column lineage check. Tests A-D and
G-H are pass/fail; E-F are documented flags. The audit reports PASS
if and only if every test succeeds.

3.7. Climate-regime generalisation (LOGO)
Three leave-one-climate-regime-out experiments (train Cold+Mixed ->
test Hot; Cold+Hot -> Mixed; Hot+Mixed -> Cold) repeat the FULL
pipeline on reduced training sets — the leakage guard, feature
engineering, and feature selection are refit from scratch on the
training regimes only. Every household is held out exactly once, so
the held-out sizes sum to the full sample (sum n_test = 18,496).
LOGO therefore doubles as a whole-population honest estimate of
cross-regime performance.

3.8. Counterfactual retrofit analysis
Retrofit scenarios edit raw survey attributes (e.g., heating fuel
switch to electric heat pump); the physics engine recomputes PH_*
features and the trained model predicts new consumption for a
1,500-household stratified subsample. Two validation layers exist:
student counterfactuals on RECS households, and simulation-derived
delta teachers trained on ResStock baseline/upgrade pairs and
applied to RECS households. We compare both layers against
composition-corrected ResStock measured deltas (Section 2.3).

4.1. Predictive performance on the held-out test set
Table 2 reports held-out test performance for every target. The
primary model attains R2 = 0.6421 in original energy units on the
untouched test split; the honest stacking ensemble reaches 0.6529.
One number misleads. We therefore report three complementary views:
the unweighted test R2 (0.6421), which treats every surveyed
household equally and is the strictest view; the NWEIGHT-weighted
test R2 (0.6711), which is the policy-relevant view because
households are weighted as they represent the national stock; and
the out-of-fold stacked R2 in log space (0.7622), the view
comparable to the log-target literature. Population calibration
error (NMBE) is -5.8%, and CV(RMSE) is approximately 40% (RMSE
33,298 kBtu against a mean consumption of 83,002 kBtu). It is worth
noting that ASHRAE Guideline 14 addresses single-building
calibration against metered data and does not apply to cross-
sectional survey prediction; we therefore report these statistics
descriptively rather than as pass/fail criteria.

End-use performance varies with physical predictability. Heating
(R2 = 0.691) couples strongly to envelope and climate; cooling
(0.589) and DHW (0.609) are moderate; lighting (0.406) is weakest,
consistent with its dependence on unobserved occupant behaviour
(Dong, Lam, Huang and Dobbs, 2021). If lighting were physics-driven,
its R2 should track envelope features; in our sample it does not.
Prediction scatter and residual heteroscedasticity are shown in
Fig. 2; feature attributions in Fig. 3.

4.2. Uncertainty calibration
Table 2's intervals deserve their own sentence. Conformal intervals
calibrated on the untouched 925-household split achieve 79.5%,
90.2%, and 96.4% empirical coverage at the 80%, 90%, and 95%
nominal levels respectively (Fig. 4) — agreement with nominal across
the full curve, tails included. The intervals are wide:
the mean 90% interval width is approximately 83,000 kBtu, on the
order of mean consumption itself. We regard this as the honest
spread of the data, not a defect of the method; a narrower interval
would imply false precision.

4.3. Ablations with seed-level confidence intervals
Because single-seed differences of a few tenths of a percentage
point are indistinguishable from noise, we test every architectural
claim across ten random seeds (42, 101, ..., 909) with 95%
confidence intervals and a sign-consistency count. Stacking is the
one intervention that survives repetition: +0.84 pp [+0.49, +1.19],
sign-consistent in all three arms, a consistent gain. The three base
learners double as mutually consistent baselines: identical
preprocessing, fixed defaults, equal optimisation effort, and the
ensemble is compared against each of them (Fig. 6). Transfer
learning does not survive. In the XGBoost arm it adds +0.07 pp
[-0.41, +0.55] (sign 7/10, within noise); in the LightGBM arm it
subtracts 0.26 pp [-0.52, -0.01] (sign 3/10), which we classify as
no measurable gain. This is a negative result. We report it as
such, and return to its interpretation in Section 5.1. The dual-
target design outperforms the single-target direct-EUI model
(Table 2). Residuals are approximately centred but heteroscedastic;
variance grows with magnitude, as expected for log-normal
consumption, which is itself an argument for conformal intervals
over constant-width bands (Fig. 2).

4.4. Climate-regime generalisation (LOGO)
Table 4 reports held-out R2 by regime. The mean R2 is 0.6978 with

4.6. Counterfactual retrofit scenarios and composition bias
Table 5 sets three estimates side by side: student counterfactuals,
simulation-derived delta teachers applied to RECS households, and
composition-corrected ResStock measured deltas — together with the
NAIVE unmatched estimates a practitioner would compute by
differencing published upgrade-file means. The signs disagree. For
air sealing (upgrade 10), which covers only 56% of the stock and
whose covered subset is compositionally unrepresentative, the naive
estimate is -8.7% while the corrected estimate is +2.1%; a
programme analyst using the naive figure would conclude that air
sealing increases consumption. The matched-cell correction
(Section 2.3) restores the correct direction, and the pipeline emits
an automatic composition-bias warning whenever the two estimates
diverge. We do not claim the corrected magnitude is exact; the
claim is narrower and, we believe, more defensible: the sign is
reliable, the magnitude carries uncertainty.

Student counterfactuals remain near zero for all scenarios
(Table 5). We read this as a boundary condition of survey-trained
models rather than a tuning failure, an observational correlation-
vs-causal-intervention gap we return to in Section 5.4.

4.7. Reproducibility
On 27 September 2026 we re-executed the entire pipeline from raw
inputs, sixteen days after the run reported above, with identical
code version, flags, and data. Both runs agree. Every headline
metric reproduced to the reported precision: primary R2 0.6421,
ensemble 0.6529, coverage 79.5/90.2/96.4%, LOGO mean 0.698, teacher
R2(log) 0.7371, and identical ten-seed verdicts; the maximum
absolute difference in any reported R2 was below 0.001. Both runs
emit identical leakage-audit verdicts (PASS, nine data tests plus
two documented flags) and machine-readable run manifests that map
every table and figure to a run stamp. Together the audit report and
the manifest act as the model card and dataset datasheet of this
pipeline, following the documentation framework recently recommended
for AI-based energy-and-buildings research (Hong and Li, 2026). On
this basis we distinguish
three layers of reproducibility and claim the first two: same-seed
re-execution (verified here), and seed-to-seed variability
(quantified by the confidence intervals of Section 4.3). Cross-
environment reproducibility — different hardware, operating systems,
or library builds — remains supported only by pinned requirements
and the archived artefacts, and we say so plainly (Section 5.6).

====================================================================
5. DISCUSSION
====================================================================
5.1. Why R2 ~ 0.65 is the honest ceiling for survey microdata
The R2 = 0.85+ figures common in simulation-only studies — where
occupant behaviour is fixed by schedule — are not attainable on
real survey microdata, and three lines of evidence bound what is.
First, cross-sectional surveys contain irreducible aleatoric
uncertainty from unobserved thermostat setpoints, window-opening
habits, and appliance usage (Dong et al., 2021). Second, our own
simulation teacher — trained on 494,974 physics-simulated buildings
with full information — plateaus at R2(log) = 0.7371; a student of
these features cannot systematically exceed the information ceiling
of the features themselves. Third, Wang and Srinivasan (2021)
document systematic degradation of deployed models relative to
simulation benchmarks, and our teacher-student comparison quantifies
that degradation directly: 0.74 in log space on simulated buildings
against 0.64-0.76, depending on the view, on surveyed ones. An R2
of 0.85 on survey-only inputs would itself be evidence of leakage —
precisely what our audit is designed to detect. Our contribution is
therefore methodological trustworthiness; the record attempt we
leave to simulation studies. The three views in Table 2 should
always be read together.

The null transfer-learning result deserves a specific kind of
attention. In our view it is the single most informative finding of the
study. The teacher is strong (R2(log) = 0.737), never sees survey
targets, and still contributes nothing measurable across ten seeds.
The most plausible reading is that ResStock's schedule-driven
behaviour model encodes information the RECS features already
capture — physics and equipment — while the residual RECS variance
is behavioural, the precise component the teacher does not model.
Alternative readings (domain shift, harmonisation loss) cannot be
excluded, and we do not pretend otherwise; a teacher retrained on
behaviour-rich simulations would discriminate between the readings.
Either way, reporting the null with confidence intervals is more
valuable to the community than a single-seed gain that vanishes
under repetition.

5.2. The lighting prediction challenge
Lighting EUI (R2 = 0.406) is weakest because lighting load is
weakly coupled to building physics and strongly dependent on
occupancy schedules, daylight availability, and personal
preferences. RECS sees none of this (Xu et al., 2025). Explicit
behavioural sensors or diary data may be required.

5.3. Climate-regime transfer
The LOGO design answers a deployment question directly relevant to
national retrofit programmes: does a model trained on some climate
regimes transfer to others? The mean R2 = 0.698 with no regime
collapse indicates generalisable physical signal rather than
regime-specific memorisation. We refuse the stronger framing. TMY3
encodes typical weather, and extreme-event risk requires design-day
or projected-climate inputs (Limitations). What the results do
support is climate-regime transfer — Cold-trained models predicting
Hot households and vice versa — which is the operationally relevant
stress test for nationally deployed stock models.

5.4. Counterfactuals: student vs. teacher, and composition bias
The student layer fails to capture retrofit sensitivity because
RECS is observational and efficient equipment is endogenous to
unobserved household characteristics; the simulation-derived teacher
isolates the physical effect through controlled baseline/upgrade
pairing — observational correlation vs. causal intervention (Chen,
Chen, Xu and Zuo, 2023). The teacher's own gaps to measured deltas
(up to ~15 pp for envelope measures, Table 5) bound what any
counterfactual layer can claim from these data.

The composition-bias result has implications beyond this pipeline.
Any analyst differencing published ResStock upgrade means against
baseline means without composition adjustment risks sign-level
errors for partially covered upgrades; air sealing is our
demonstration case. The matched-cell correction is simple, requires
no proprietary identifiers, and ships with the pipeline. Future
work will explore instrumental-variable or matching approaches for
causal identification on the survey side.

5.5. Implications for stock-level policy and programme evaluation
Three results speak directly to stock-level decision-making. First,
calibrated intervals turn a stock model into a risk instrument:
programme targeting and retrofit financing can condition on the
interval rather than the point, so capital is not allocated on false
precision. Second, the composition-bias warning concerns every
analyst who differences published ResStock upgrade means against the
baseline: for partially covered upgrades the naive estimator can
fail at the sign level, and the matched-cell correction we ship
requires no proprietary identifiers. Third, the honest ceiling is
itself policy-relevant: models used for code design or programme
evaluation should be benchmarked against what survey microdata can
support, and the energy-performance-gap literature shows why
unvalidated predictions matter — predicted savings systematically
exceed realised savings, with documented financial and, in extreme
retrofit cases, physical consequences (Menezes, Cripps, Bouchlaghem
and Buswell, 2012; ABCB / DCCEEW, 2019). Finally, the electrification
scenarios (cold-climate ASHP) connect the pipeline to decarbonisation
analysis, with the caveat that site-to-source carbon factors would
be required for full carbon accounting (Section 5.6).

5.6. Limitations
Six limitations deserve explicit mention, stated where the claims
are made as well as here. First, the model predicts site energy
rather than carbon-weighted source energy; grid-region intensity
factors would be required for full decarbonisation analysis.
Second, the cross-sectional design precludes temporal validation.
Third, the physics-informed features rely on simplified geometry and
thermodynamic proxies derived from survey codes; they are not full
building-energy simulations. Fourth, ResStock teachers simplify
occupant behaviour through fixed schedules — likely the reason the
transfer contributes no measurable gain (Section 5.1). Fifth, the
leakage audit cannot exclude every subtle leakage mode, the linear-
combination test is capped at four-feature groups, and the heating
routing decision was a 0.05 pp knife-edge we have disclosed as such
(Section 3.3). Sixth, TMY3 represents typical rather than extreme
weather, the study covers the United States only, and cross-
environment reproducibility rests on pinned requirements and
archived artefacts rather than a second-hardware verification
(Section 4.7).

====================================================================
6. CONCLUSIONS
====================================================================
The pipeline we presented is a measuring instrument as much as a
predictor: its audit, its untouched calibration split, and its seed
intervals exist so that every claim above can be re-run, bounded, or
refuted on another machine. The number a reader should carry away
is 0.64 — the share of variance in total site energy that an
audited model explains on 18,496 surveyed households, in original
units, with intervals that keep their promise at three nominal
levels. The remaining 36% is where unobserved occupant behaviour
lives; no teacher trained on 494,974 simulated buildings moved it,
and no configuration we tested moves it until surveys measure
behaviour. That residue, not the architecture, is the honest limit
of this evidence.
